What each part does

Resistor v = i·R

A narrow path that resists the flow. The more wire the current has to pass through, the more voltage it takes to push the same current: Ohm's law. The energy it takes does not come back; it leaves as heat. Slide the wiper along the winding and watch the thermometer.

Inductor v = L·di/dt

A coil makes a magnetic field when current runs through it, and the field holds energy. A steady current is fine, but changing it means changing the field, and the coil pushes back against the change. That is why current cannot jump: it has to build up. A ferrite slug concentrates the field, so pushing it in raises L. The compass beside the coil swings with the field.

Capacitor i = C·dv/dt

Two plates with a gap. Charge piles onto one plate and is drawn off the other, but nothing crosses the gap, so the carriers on the wire stop at the plates. The charge sets up an electric field across the gap, and the voltage is v = q/C. More overlap means more room for charge, so more C. Its voltage follows the charge, so it cannot jump either.

All three in one loop vs = vR + vL + vC

The same current flows through all three, and their voltages add up to the source. The coil and the capacitor trade energy: the current builds the magnetic field, the field keeps the current going and charges the capacitor, then the capacitor pushes it back. The resistor drains a bit each swing. Raise R until the ringing stops: that is critical damping, at R = 2·√(L/C).
